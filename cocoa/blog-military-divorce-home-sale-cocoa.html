Military Divorce and Home Sales in Brevard County

Navigating property division when military life meets divorce

Home → Blog → Military Divorce Home Sale

Military life in Brevard County comes with unique rewards. Proximity to Patrick Space Force Base, a tight-knit community of servicemembers and families, and the pride of serving the nation. But military marriages also face extraordinary stress: frequent deployments, long separations, PCS moves every few years, and the demands of military culture. When a military marriage ends in divorce, the challenges multiply — especially when it comes to selling the family home.

A military divorce involves layers of complexity that civilian divorces do not. Federal laws intersect with Florida family law. VA loans come with special rules. The Servicemembers Civil Relief Act (SCRA) affects timelines and protections. And the emotional weight of ending a marriage while one or both spouses serve their country adds a burden that is difficult to describe.

If you are facing military divorce in Brevard County and need to sell your home in Cocoa, Rockledge, Merritt Island, or Titusville, this guide will help you understand your rights, your options, and the fastest path to resolution.

Unique Challenges of Military Divorce

Before diving into the specifics of home sales, it is important to understand why military divorce differs from civilian divorce. These differences affect everything from jurisdiction to property division to the timeline for selling your home.

Jurisdiction and Where to File

In a civilian divorce, jurisdiction is usually straightforward: you file in the state where you live. For military families, jurisdiction can be complicated. You may be able to file in:

  • The state where the military spouse is stationed (Florida, if stationed at Patrick SFB)
  • The state where the non-military spouse resides
  • The state where the military spouse claims legal residency
  • The state where you were married

Each state has different laws regarding property division, alimony, and child custody. Choosing the right jurisdiction is critical, and a mistake can cost you significantly. Consult an attorney who specializes in military divorce before filing.

The Impact of Deployment on Divorce Proceedings

If one spouse is deployed or stationed overseas, divorce proceedings can be delayed. Under SCRA, courts must grant a stay of proceedings for at least 90 days if the servicemember's military duties prevent them from participating. While this protection exists for good reason — no one should lose their home while defending the country — it can extend the timeline for selling your property.

Dividing Military Benefits and Pensions

Military retirement pay is often one of the largest marital assets. Under the Uniformed Services Former Spouses' Protection Act (USFSPA), state courts can divide military retirement pay as marital property. The 10/10 rule determines whether the Defense Finance and Accounting Service (DFAS) will send payments directly to the former spouse. This complexity can affect negotiations over the home: one spouse may fight harder to keep the house if they are losing a share of retirement pay.

SCRA Protections and Your Home Sale

The Servicemembers Civil Relief Act is one of the most important federal laws affecting military divorce and home sales. SCRA provides several protections that can delay or complicate a sale — but also protections that may benefit you.

SCRA Interest Rate Cap

If your mortgage interest rate exceeds 6% and you entered active duty after taking out the loan, SCRA may cap your rate at 6% during your active-duty service. This reduction can lower your monthly payments while you decide whether to sell, keep, or transfer the home. Contact your lender and provide a copy of your orders to request the reduction.

Foreclosure Protection

SCRA protects active-duty servicemembers from foreclosure without a court order. Lenders cannot foreclose on your Cocoa home while you are on active duty — and for a period after — without obtaining judicial approval. This protection gives you breathing room if financial strain during divorce makes mortgage payments difficult.

Default Judgment Protection

If your spouse files for divorce while you are deployed or unable to appear due to military service, SCRA prevents the court from entering a default judgment against you without appointing an attorney to represent your interests. While this protection is essential for fairness, it can delay the divorce and, by extension, any court-ordered home sale.

VA Loans and Military Divorce

Many military families in Brevard County purchase homes using VA loans, which offer zero-down financing and competitive rates. But VA loans create unique complications during divorce.

VA Loan Assumption

Unlike most conventional loans, VA loans are assumable. This means a qualified buyer — including the non-military spouse or a third-party purchaser — can take over the existing loan terms. However, VA loan assumptions are not automatic. The assuming party must meet credit and income requirements, and the VA must approve the assumption.

If one spouse wants to keep the home, a VA loan assumption may be the cleanest solution. The remaining spouse assumes the loan, refinances if necessary, and the departing spouse is released from liability. However, assumptions can take 30 to 45 days, and if the assuming spouse does not qualify, the home must be sold or refinanced into a conventional loan.

Restoration of Entitlement

VA loan entitlement is a precious resource for military homebuyers. If you used your entitlement to purchase your Cocoa home, you may want it restored so you can buy again at your next duty station. Entitlement restoration typically requires either selling the home and paying off the VA loan or having another qualified veteran assume the loan.

In a military divorce, the division of VA loan responsibility directly affects your ability to use this benefit in the future. Make sure your divorce decree clearly specifies who is responsible for the mortgage and under what conditions entitlement will be restored.

Funding Fee Considerations

If you refinance a VA loan during or after divorce, you may be required to pay the VA funding fee again. For regular military, this fee ranges from 1.4% to 3.6% of the loan amount. Factor this cost into your calculations when deciding whether to sell, assume, or refinance.

Dividing the Family Home in a Military Divorce

Florida's equitable distribution laws apply to military divorces just as they do to civilian ones. The family home is a marital asset subject to fair division. However, military life introduces factors that courts must consider.

The Role of BAH in Property Division

Basic Allowance for Housing (BAH) is a significant component of military compensation. While BAH is technically intended to cover housing costs, courts may consider it as income when determining property division, alimony, and child support. If one spouse receives BAH at the "with dependents" rate while married, a divorce may reduce that rate to "without dependents," affecting their ability to afford the mortgage alone.

Courts in Brevard County may consider whether the home was purchased based on BAH income, whether the servicemember will continue receiving BAH at the same rate, and whether the non-military spouse has sufficient income to contribute to housing costs post-divorce.

The 20/20/20 and 20/20/15 Rules

These rules determine whether a former military spouse retains certain benefits after divorce, including TRICARE and commissary privileges. While they do not directly affect the home sale, they influence the overall financial picture:

  • 20/20/20: 20 years of marriage, 20 years of military service, and 20 years of overlap. The former spouse keeps full TRICARE and commissary benefits.
  • 20/20/15: 20 years of marriage, 20 years of service, and 15 to 19 years of overlap. The former spouse receives one year of transitional TRICARE.

A spouse who loses TRICARE coverage may face significant new healthcare costs, affecting their ability to buy out the home or qualify for a new mortgage.

Why a Cash Sale Is Ideal for Military Divorce

Military divorce is already complicated enough without adding a protracted home sale to the mix. A cash sale offers specific advantages for divorcing military families in Brevard County.

Speed Reduces Conflict

The longer a home sale drags on, the more opportunity there is for conflict between divorcing spouses. Disagreements over repairs, price reductions, and buyer selection can reignite emotional battles. A cash sale that closes in 10 to 14 days minimizes these opportunities for conflict.

No Need for Both Spouses to Cooperate Extensively

In a traditional sale, both spouses must sign disclosures, attend inspections, and agree on every negotiation point. If one spouse is deployed, stationed elsewhere, or simply refusing to cooperate, the sale stalls. Cash buyers streamline this process, often requiring minimal involvement from both parties once the initial agreement is signed.

Certainty for PCS-Bound Spouses

If one spouse has received PCS orders and must relocate soon, a guaranteed closing date is essential. Cash buyers can close before the servicemember reports to their new duty station, ensuring the home sale does not interfere with military obligations.

Preserve VA Entitlement for Future Purchases

Selling the home and paying off the VA loan is the cleanest way to restore entitlement for both spouses. A fast cash sale ensures the loan is satisfied quickly, allowing the servicemember to use their VA benefit at their next assignment and the former spouse to pursue their own housing goals without lingering financial ties.

Working with Attorneys and the Court

Selling a home during military divorce requires careful coordination with your attorney and, in some cases, the court. Here are key considerations:

  • Get court approval if necessary: If the divorce is pending and the home is a marital asset, you may need court approval to sell. Your attorney can file a motion for sale or negotiate a stipulation with your spouse's attorney.
  • Clear title issues early: Military moves sometimes result in title irregularities. Ensure both spouses are properly on the deed and that any power of attorney documents are valid and recorded.
  • Address tax implications: The capital gains exclusion for primary residences ($250,000 for single filers, $500,000 for married couples) may be affected by your divorce filing status in the year of sale. Consult a tax professional.
  • Protect your credit: Until the home is sold and the mortgage paid off, both spouses remain liable. If one spouse stops paying, both credit scores suffer. A fast sale eliminates this risk.
